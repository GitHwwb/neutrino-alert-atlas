      Why only IceCube? What about KM3NeT and Baikal-GVD?

      Four operating high-energy neutrino telescopes are marked on the map: IceCube at the South Pole, KM3NeT/ARCA and KM3NeT/ORCA in the Mediterranean (off Sicily and Toulon), and Baikal-GVD in Russia's Lake Baikal.

      Of these, only IceCube currently publishes a structured public candidate-alert catalog. Its Gold and Bronze alerts go to the NASA GCN AMON feed as a machine-readable table updated within minutes of detection — that's the source this Atlas ingests.

      KM3NeT is still in phased deployment (~28 of 230 planned detection units installed for ARCA as of 2025) and has issued only a handful of GCN circulars to date, mostly upper-limit follow-ups to LIGO and GRB events. The collaboration announced a single ~220 PeV event (KM3-230213A, detected Feb 2023) via a Nature paper in February 2025 — the highest-energy neutrino ever observed — but that was a one-off, not part of a routine real-time alert stream. Baikal-GVD publishes some events through GCN circulars but does not have a parsable catalog.

      So ingesting other detectors today would mean parsing free-text circulars (low yield, high effort) or hand-curating from peer-reviewed papers. When KM3NeT or Baikal-GVD register a structured GCN Notice schema, they'll be much easier to add — the geometry pipeline here is detector-agnostic.

      Why do most entry points cluster in the southern hemisphere?

      At the South Pole, source declination largely determines the latitude where an up-going trajectory enters Earth. The Atlas computes that intersection directly on the WGS84 ellipsoid from each event's Earth-fixed source vector rather than using a spherical approximation.

      The IceCube alert population skews to low-to-mid northern declinations (≈ 0°–30°) because two effects compete: sources at negative declination appear down-going at the South Pole and don't enter the up-going filter; sources at high positive declination require the neutrino to cross more of Earth's diameter, where absorption at PeV energies cuts the effective area. The sweet spot for through-Earth detection is near the celestial equator. Mapped back through the geometry, that puts the entry points between roughly −90° and −30° latitude.

      How does IceCube tell a down-going neutrino from cosmic-ray background?

      IceCube is a cubic kilometer of Antarctic ice instrumented with 5,160 light sensors. When a neutrino interacts with a molecule in the ice it produces a charged particle that travels faster than light moves through ice, creating a faint blue Cherenkov glow. The sensors time those photons; from that pattern, IceCube reconstructs the particle's track and energy.

      Up-going events are easy. If something is moving upward through the detector it had to come from below — through the entire Earth. Earth absorbs essentially everything except neutrinos. So any high-energy track going up is almost certainly a neutrino.

      Down-going candidates are hard. Cosmic rays slam into the upper atmosphere above Antarctica all the time, producing a constant rain of muons that look superficially similar to neutrino tracks. IceCube separates candidate neutrino tracks from this huge background using event topology, containment, and energy-dependent background estimates.

      • Using containment and veto information. Light recorded before the reconstructed track reaches the detector can identify an entering atmospheric muon.
      • Looking at energy. Atmospheric backgrounds fall steeply with energy, so a high-energy track is less background-like.
      • Reconstructing track quality. Timing and spatial consistency across the detector constrain the direction and reject poorly reconstructed backgrounds.

      Gold/Bronze candidate alerts meet the pipeline's signalness and false-alarm-rate thresholds, so they can be circulated to follow-up telescopes within minutes. They are not confirmations of astrophysical origin.

      What's the "AGM2015 ν̄ flux" overlay?

      Toggling this overlays the actual AGM2015 antineutrino global map from Usman, Jocher, Dye, McDonough & Learned (Sci. Rep. 5, 13945, 2015) — an experimentally informed model of Earth's surface antineutrino flux over the 0–11 MeV band. The bright blue regions are dominated by reactor flux; the surrounding warmer gradient is the geoneutrino contribution from U/Th decay in the continental crust and mantle. The channel selector switches between the total, reactor-only, and geological-only renderings.

      This is the real AGM2015 imagery from the authors' code repository, not a derivation. The dropdown lets you isolate the reactor component (hotspots over nuclear-dense regions) from the geological component (broad continental gradient — note the "dark patches" around mountain ranges where the crust is thick).

      Important: this is a totally different physical regime from the TeV–PeV astrophysical events on this map. The reactor and geoneutrino antineutrinos are ~109–1012 times lower in energy and come from terrestrial sources, not cosmic ones. The overlay is shown only as geographic context — "where on Earth is antineutrino production strongest, regardless of energy."

      • Catalog scope: public IceCube Gold/Bronze candidate alerts since June 2019, plus one hand-curated KM3NeT event. The observed IceCube rate in this catalog is roughly 27 active candidates per year, not a per-location detection rate.
      • Closest-approach distance is a geometric reconstruction: the shortest distance from the observer's WGS84 position to the finite atmospheric-entry-to-detector segment. It is not a measurement at the observer's location.
      • Direction uncertainty means the trajectory is a corridor, not an exact line. Use each event's listed 90% localization radius when interpreting a closest-approach value; the catalog contains track alerts, not a uniform angular-resolution class.
      • IceCube's alert stream targets high-energy candidates. Solar neutrino flux at Earth is of order 1011 per cm² per second, but those much lower-energy neutrinos lie outside this catalog.
